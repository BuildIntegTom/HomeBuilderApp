Here’s the outline I’d use. It goes from the forces the building puts on the wall, to how each panel carries them, to proving the numbers to a building official.
1. Define the demand
Work out wind and seismic loads under ASCE 7 for the building types you sell: risk category, wind speed and exposure, and Seismic Design Category. 
Distribute those loads through the roof and floor diaphragms to each braced wall line. The result is a design shear per wall line (lb/ft), plus overturning at each line. 
Choose ASD or LRFD now and stick with it. Load duration factor C_D = 1.6 applies to wind and seismic in ASD. 
2. Define the panel and its load path
Draw the actual geometry: plates, studs, diagonals, where the diagonals meet, and every connection. 
Trace the load path: diaphragm → top plate or collector → braces → chords (end studs) → bottom plate → anchor bolts and hold-downs → foundation. Each link needs a number. 
Decide what the straw is assumed to do. For the analysis, assume it carries nothing. Test later to see whether it adds stiffness or holds the braces against buckling. 
3. Idealize the panel as a truss and solve the forces
Treat it as a pin-jointed truss. A unit horizontal load V at the top gives the brace forces, chord tension and compression (roughly V·h/L), and the plate forces. 
The main geometric advantage of a K: each diagonal spans only half the height, so it sits at a shallower angle. Brace force scales with 1/cos θ. On a 1.2 × 2.7 m panel, a single full-height diagonal is at about 66° (force ≈ 2.5V). A K diagonal is at about 48°, which roughly halves the force per brace. 
The main weakness: the diagonals meet partway up a stud, not at a corner. If one brace buckles or its joint slips, the stud takes a sideways load at mid-height and has to resist it in bending. Check that case explicitly. 
Load reverses, so every brace must work in both tension and compression. 
4. Check the members (NDS)
Tension: net section at bolt holes and fastener groups. 
Compression: column stability (C_P) using the brace’s unbraced length. 
Studs: combined bending and axial load where the K joint lands mid-height, added to their gravity load. 
Plates: tension and compression as collectors. 
5. Design the connections (usually what governs)
Brace-end joints using nails, screws, bolts or metal plates, sized with the NDS yield equations. Check end and edge distances, the number of fasteners, and any eccentricity where members meet off-center. 
Avoid joints that rely on toe-nailing or on screws pulling straight out of the wood (withdrawal). 
Size the hold-downs for chord uplift (net of dead load), anchor bolts for sliding, and the panel-to-panel splines that transfer shear along the wall. 
6. Check stiffness and drift
Panel deflection = truss member deformation + connection slip. In timber trusses the slip dominates, often by a wide margin. The NDS and Eurocode 5 both give load-slip values for fasteners. 
Compare the story drift to the ASCE 7 limits. Also compare the panel’s stiffness to the plywood walls it might share a wall line with, because stiffer walls attract more of the load. 
7. Confirm the code path for seismic (the biggest non-math hurdle)
Wood shear walls sheathed with structural panels are a listed system in ASCE 7 Table 12.2-1, with R = 6.5. As far as I know, K-braced timber frames aren’t listed. Possible routes: 
Wind-governed or low-SDC markets: an engineered design under the IBC alternative-means provision (104.11) may be enough. 
Unlisted-system category: use a conservative category such as “shear panels of all other materials,” which has a low R and height limits. 
Full qualification: run a test program like the ICC-ES AC130 approach for prefabricated wood shear panels, which is how products such as Simpson Strong-Wall got listed, or the FEMA P695 method for a new system. 
Ask EcoCocon what their ETA (European technical approval) is based on. It may cover only Eurocode 5 analysis and wind. Their testing approach would be a useful model. 
8. Test to confirm and calibrate
Connection tests: load-slip curves and capacity for your actual brace joints. 
Full-scale racking tests (ASTM E564 static, ASTM E2126 cyclic): with and without straw, and with plaster if it’s always present. 
From the tests, get capacity, stiffness, ductility and failure mode, then derive allowable shear per panel with the right safety factors. Compare against your current plywood or strap panels. 
9. Package the results
A design table for each panel type: allowable shear, stiffness, hold-down force, required connections, and any aspect-ratio limits. 
An engineer of record signs it, and an evaluation report follows if you pursue one.

What happens if you use screws
Strength is adequate. A structural wood screw loaded sideways through a 1½ in member into SPF carries roughly 150–250 lb ASD after the 1.6 factor, depending on diameter and the product’s evaluation report. So it takes about 3–4 screws per brace end at 570 lb. 
Stiffness is the weakness. Screws loaded sideways have to slip before they pick up load. That slip happens at all eight brace ends and turns into racking drift. Expect the panel to be noticeably softer than the truss analysis predicts, and softer than a plywood wall. That matters for drift, and if K panels share a wall line with plywood panels, the stiffer plywood takes more than its share of the load. 
Load reversal: screws loaded sideways loosen gradually as the wood crushes under repeated wind cycles. That’s why cyclic testing (ASTM E2126) matters. 
Splitting: a screw near the end of a diagonal cut at a steep angle is close to the end grain. Use self-drilling structural screws with their published minimum end and edge distances. 
Better options, roughly in order of cost-effectiveness for your line
Bearing-cut ends plus screws (recommended starting point). Cut each diagonal end to fit snugly against the stud and plate (a double miter into the corner, and a matching seat at the center stud). Compression then travels by wood-on-wood bearing, which is stiff and doesn’t slip, so the screws only carry tension. You need accurate saw cuts and a jig for the screw pattern. No special tooling. 
Screws driven at an angle (European practice). Fully threaded screws driven at about 45° across the joint are loaded along their length, not sideways. They’re several times stiffer and stronger per screw, and they’re how much European timber prefab is done, likely including EcoCocon. Several are available in the US with ICC-ES reports, e.g. Rothoblaas VGZ, SWG ASSY and Simpson SDWS/SDCF. The design values come from each product’s evaluation report, not the NDS tables. 
Plywood or OSB gussets. A ½ in gusset on the inner face of each frame, nailed with a pneumatic nailer, optionally with construction adhesive. It’s cheap, there’s no conflict with finishes because the gusset sits in the straw cavity, and it’s very stiff, especially glued. The cost is labor and handling. Rated sheathing with nails has well-established design values. 
Light-gauge steel straps (e.g. Simpson LSTA/MSTA). Strap over the joint to take tension, with bearing-cut ends for compression. Inexpensive and code-recognized, but less stiff than the options above. 
My recommendation: prototype option 1, compare it in a few racking tests against option 2 (angled screws), and keep plywood gussets as a fallback. Option 1 also suits your production line well, since accurate end cuts are something a CNC or a stop-blocked saw does cheaply.

What this means for screws
The photo shows that the chords, plates and diagonals all lie in one plane, flush with each other. No member overlaps another, so you can’t screw through one member’s face into another the normal way, which is how screws carry load well. Your choices are:
Ordinary screws: toe-screwed, or driven through the chord into the end grain of the diagonal. Both are weak and slip a lot. NDS gives no design value for withdrawal from end grain. I wouldn’t use this for the main load path. 
Inclined fully threaded screws: driven through the chord or plate at an angle into the diagonal, so they cross the joint and are loaded along their length. This is how European prefab handles flush joints and is likely what EcoCocon uses. It’s strong and stiff, with design values from the product’s evaluation report (e.g. Rothoblaas VGZ, SWG ASSY VG, Simpson SDWS/SDCF). Each one needs a jig to set the angle, but no press. 
Face plates screwed or nailed on (no press needed): flat steel straps or tie plates (Simpson ST/MSTA/LSTA-type), or ½ in plywood gussets on the inner face of each frame. These carry load the efficient way, through the face, and use standard cataloged values. There’s no conflict with finishes because they face the straw cavity.

Things to be aware of
One-sided plate: the force acts about ¾ in off the member centerline, which twists the joint out of plane. At 690 lb that’s about 540 in-lb, which the 36 in through-thickness braces and the straw can easily resist. Pressed truss plates are always installed on both faces for this reason, so this detail should be confirmed by test. 
Stiffness: using the NDS load/slip modulus for wood-to-metal (270,000 D^1.5 ≈ 15,400 lb/in per nail), nail slip plus truss deformation gives roughly 0.15–0.2 in of top-of-panel drift at design load (4-panel case), plus hold-down slip. That’s comparable to a plywood wall at the same unit shear, a good sign for mixing K panels with plywood panels. Real slip under cyclic load is usually larger, so racking tests will give the true figure.
